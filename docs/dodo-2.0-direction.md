# DODO 2.0

*An opportunity note · for Jacob · Draft for discussion · August 2026 · Private*

**DODO 1.0: District Door Opener — the engine**
**DODO 2.0: District-Originated, District-Owned — the addition**

A decade of relationships on the education side has produced something nobody else in K-12 has: the trust of the people who run districts, on a first-name, text-message basis, coast to coast. This note is about a second thing that asset can do — a market that is forming right now, that has no broker, and that only one person is positioned to broker.

> 🦤 **Why the bird fits better than ever.** Dodos were famously trusting — and in this market, being the most trusted creature on the island *is* the business model. This note is about a second island for the same bird.

---

## 1. A market is forming with no broker in it

Something new is happening inside districts. Their teams have gotten genuinely good — the modern district office builds small tools with AI, runs its own pilots, and knows its needs better than any outsider ever has. Which means districts are quietly accumulating a new kind of inventory: **tools they've built, playbooks they've proven, and expertise their peers keep calling about.**

That inventory has real buyers — other districts — and today it moves through exactly one channel: hallway conversations at conferences, county-office meetings, and texts between superintendents. No pricing, no packaging, no mechanism. An entire district-to-district economy running on friendship and luck.

Here's the thing about that informal channel: **Jacob is already it.** He's the person superintendents call to ask "who's figured this out?" The opportunity is simply to make the thing he already does — connecting the education side to what it needs — work in a second direction: district to district, with real economics attached.

> And this lane has a property the first one never had: **every move in it is a give.** "Your peer built something for exactly this — want to see it?" is a gift, not a request. A whole business line where every touch makes the network warmer.

## 2. Jacob's network is already shaped for this

Three things about his own roster, each one an unfair advantage:

**The top of K-12 can't sell — and he can.** Sitting superintendents and principals structurally cannot look like sellers: ethics rules, procurement law, board optics, peer reputation. So everything they build and know stays parked — a mountain of proven, educator-built value with no way to reach the market on its own. The service only a trusted broker can offer: **Jacob is the salesperson so they never have to be.** Nobody else has the standing to make that offer.

**He's already invented the key instrument.** Jacob pays a district director an hourly rate to sit on company calls — practitioner expertise, purchased cleanly, repeatable forever. That arrangement, scaled to two sides, is an expertise brokerage: districts and companies paying for practitioner time — advisory panels, pilot design, honest teardowns — with Jacob taking a cut. Advisory pay is reputation-safe in a way commission never is. He's already run the pilot; this just gives it a storefront.

**The roster is full of the word "Former."** Former supe, former ED, former state president, former conference head — everywhere in his contacts. Formers carry full credibility, zero optics constraints, no conflicts, and free time. That's not a list of past relationships. It's an unsigned talent agency waiting for an agent.

## 3. The intelligence only Jacob can assemble

Everyone in ed-tech knows what districts *need* — needs are commodity knowledge. What nobody holds is the **attempt history**: for each district and each need, what they've tried — bought, built, borrowed from a peer, abandoned — at what cost, with what outcome, and why. That's the layer that says which peer-built tool would transfer, which expertise is worth brokering, and where the real openings are.

**District → Need → Attempt/Outcome → Fit**

The beautiful part: most of that layer is sitting in public — board minutes record every purchase and every renewal, budgets and plans state priorities in the district's own words, job postings announce needs out loud. It's collectable, and it refreshes itself. What the public record can never contain is the *why* — and the why is exactly what flows through Jacob's phone every day. Public data as the substrate, his judgment as the seasoning: a machine anyone could start, that only he can finish.

Bonus: the same intelligence sharpens everything he already does. An introduction aimed with attempt histories lands better — for everyone he works with, on every side.

## 4. Three engines, one flywheel

The addition comes as three engines that feed each other:

1. **Fiduciary — paid from savings, a budget line every district has.** Stack rationalization and pooled renewals: review a district's 200-tool sprawl, retire the bottom third, renegotiate the rest as a bloc across many districts, share the savings. Districts get money back, Jacob becomes their trusted advisor on day one — and the work itself generates the attempt-history graph as a side effect.

2. **Exchange — success fees on real fit.** Aggregate demand across the network: "eleven districts share this precise need; two have partial in-house builds; here's the spec." When a solution exists — peer-built or vendor — broker the match and take a success fee on district-initiated deals. Pre-qualified fit, priced as the product it is.

3. **Studio — when nothing fits, build it: district-originated, district-owned.** Every unmet-need spec from the exchange is deal flow. Nothing gets built that five districts haven't already asked for — demand is proven before a line of code, and the pilot districts are pre-committed because they're the ones who raised the need.

The loop: audits feed the graph → the graph aims the exchange → unmet demand feeds the studio → pilot evidence flows back into the graph. The loop is the moat.

## 5. The Studio — three benches he already owns

The talent for this is already in his phone. The most technical people in his network — the Rob Dickensons, the Patricks, the CTOs who already ship real tools for their own districts — are a supply side nobody has ever organized. And his favorite formers are the one sales force no vendor can hire: superintendents presenting to superintendents.

- **Builders (supply)** — the technical supes and CTOs who already ship. They build v1s; the studio hardens them into products.
- **Champions (distribution)** — the top formers. They kill weak projects and present strong ones peer-to-peer. "Built by superintendents" is the one pitch nobody else can make.
- **Exchange (aim)** — the attempt graph decides *which* of a builder's ten tools is the one fifty districts need — so the studio only builds winners.

Builder builds, champion sells, exchange aims. These three groups already know each other socially — there has just never been a commercial mechanism between them. **Jacob's brokerage is that mechanism.**

### What the studio actually does

It's not a dev shop. "Works in my district" becomes a product through the unglamorous 20% no practitioner-builder wants to do: multi-tenancy, security review, student-privacy agreements state by state, SSO and rostering integrations, hosting, a support desk. The studio is a **productization and liability wrapper** around people who already build — faster and cheaper than building anything from scratch.

- **IP comes from the district, not the person.** What an employee builds on district time belongs to the district — so the studio licenses the tool from the district, and royalties flow back to it. Clean for everyone, and a story a superintendent can proudly tell a board.
- **Founder-in-residence as the path.** "Come build this full-time: your district keeps a royalty, you keep founder equity." Today, when technical district people outgrow the seat, a vendor hires them away. The studio gives that talent somewhere better to land — inside Jacob's orbit.
- **Active builders stay reputation-safe.** Authorship and district-routed royalties while in seat, convertible to equity on exit. Champions carry the selling.
- **Small, focused, workflow-shaped products.** Compliance automation, board-doc drafting, sub-scheduling, MTSS paperwork, grant discovery. Practical tools for the people who know exactly what practical means.
- **Evidence built in.** Every pilot produces a real outcomes write-up, ideally with a university partner — the currency (peer proof, independent research, board optics) this profession actually values.
- **Kill discipline.** Sunset half after pilot, cheerfully. The test for any candidate tool: does it survive its author leaving the room?

## 6. How the money works

Pricing doctrine first, because it's the unlock: **price everything under board-approval and RFP thresholds** — roughly $5–20k per district per year depending on state. Below the threshold, a superintendent can simply buy it. High volume, low price, near-zero sales cost — possible because building is now cheap and distribution rides on peer trust, which is exactly what this structure has.

| Stream | Who pays | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| Product ARR | Districts | $5–20k/yr per district, under-threshold. The core engine. |
| Founding co-development | The anchor district | Pays part of the build from its existing pain budget → gets the product at cost forever, **plus a royalty when other districts buy**. "Pay to found, earn when it spreads" — a budget line becomes an asset. |
| Services wrap | Districts | Implementation and coaching from the champions' bench at day rates; brokered, studio takes ~20–25%. |
| Sponsorship | Foundations · states · brands | Non-dilutive cohort funding. "Educator-led R&D" is squarely what foundations fund; brand partners underwrite cohorts for halo. |
| Exits | Major vendors | A product with authentic adoption + evidence + a peer install base is an acquisition target. The studio becomes the farm system for the majors. |
| Exchange fees | Vendors | Success fees on district-initiated matches — kept structurally separate from the studio (see the guardrails). |

**Cap table per product** (each solution its own line, never one blob company): studio ~50% · builder-founder 25–35% · champion 10–15% sweat · origin district 5–10% royalty · active contributors paid in authorship and district royalty only.

**Order-of-magnitude economics:** $40–80k to first pilot per solution — less when the studio is hardening an existing district-built v1. A cohort of 6–8 a year on well under $1M operating. Kill half. One survivor reaching 40 districts at ~$10k is ~$400k ARR at software margins with almost no sales cost. A few survivors compounding across two cohorts puts portfolio revenue in the low millions by year three — before services, sponsorship, or an exit — and every dollar of it arrives while making the network warmer.

## 7. Guardrails — protecting the trust that makes it work

The whole machine runs on Jacob's standing with districts, so the guardrails are the design center, not an afterthought:

1. Studio products enter the exchange **labeled**, and win only on evidence rank — never placement.
2. The advisory side never recommends a studio product without **written disclosure**.
3. Under-threshold pricing keeps every purchase small enough that nobody's career rides on it.
4. Vendors pay only on **district-initiated** deals.
5. Active educators are compensated only through **district-routed, disclosed** channels, never commission.

The deeper protection is structural: because products are district-originated and royalties flow back, the studio reads as the network's own R&D lab. That story holds as long as the royalty checks are real.

**Eyes-open notes:** some district-built tools are duct tape whose real runtime is the author's presence — the champions' kill rate is the quality bar. The maintenance tail is the silent cost — one standardized stack, ruthless sunsetting. And builders love their tools — a former supe saying "this one isn't it" lands; a broker saying it doesn't.

## 8. Finding out costs five phone calls

The whole idea can be tested in a week, by Jacob, on the phone — with people who already love hearing from him:

- **To his three most technical builders:** "Walk me through everything you've built that another district has asked to copy — and would you take equity in it and go full-time if your district kept a royalty?"
- **To his three favorite former supes:** "What did you figure out that peers still call you about — and would you present the best of these tools to twenty superintendents, with a stake in what spreads?"
- **To two districts he's close to:** "Would you put $25k of your existing pain budget into having this solved, if it came back at cost — with a royalty to you when it spreads?"
- **To two of his current companies:** "Would you pay a success fee for a district-initiated, pre-qualified deal instead of a retainer for introductions?"

The inventory from the first call — the list of tools other districts already asked to copy — *is the studio's founding portfolio.* Five yeses, and this stops being a concept and becomes a cap table waiting for paperwork.

Jacob keeps opening district doors for companies, and starts opening district doors for other districts too. Same bird, new feathers.
